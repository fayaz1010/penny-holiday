Planning an overseas trip from Australia is exciting, but exchange rates, airline fees and unexpected admin costs can quickly stretch your budget. Whether you're starting with the basics of cheap international holidays from Australia or fine-tuning your next itinerary, a structured approach to pre-trip planning helps your money go further. Small changes made before you book flights or arrive at the airport often deliver the biggest savings. This guide covers practical money-saving strategies for banking, travel insurance, airfares and the admin costs every Australian traveller should plan for.

Quick answers

  • What's the quickest way to save on overseas purchases? Pay with a card that has no foreign transaction fees (such as Macquarie's debit card or ING Orange Everyday) and always choose to pay in the local currency rather than Australian dollars.
  • Does travel insurance cover any destination? No. Policies commonly exclude destinations where Smartraveller advises "Do not travel", and may refuse claims linked to riding without the right licence or excluded high-risk activities. Check your PDS.
  • How can I get the lowest airfares? Use price-beat guarantees (like Jetstar's), watch major sales, and combine sale fares with airline vouchers, travel credits or frequent flyer points where the terms allow.

1. How can Australian travellers avoid high bank fees and poor exchange rates overseas?

Use a card with no foreign transaction fees and always choose to pay in the local currency at payment terminals and ATMs.

Many standard Australian bank cards charge a foreign transaction fee of around 3% on overseas purchases. On a trip where you spend $3,000 on your card, a 3% fee adds up to about $90 – money you could have spent on your holiday.

Options worth comparing (check each provider's current terms before you sign up): * Macquarie Transaction Account: Macquarie says it charges no international fees on overseas purchases (in store or online) and no fees for overseas ATM withdrawals with its debit card – though overseas ATM operators may charge their own fees. Foreign currency purchases are converted using the Mastercard exchange rate. * ING Orange Everyday: Since 15 October 2025, ING says all Orange Everyday customers automatically get no ING international transaction fees and no ING international ATM withdrawal fees, without having to meet monthly conditions. Third-party ATM operator fees aren't rebated. * Multi-currency travel cards (such as Wise): These let you hold and convert many currencies, usually at or close to the mid-market rate plus a disclosed conversion fee that varies by currency. Check the provider's pricing page.

Watch out for Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC) too. When you pay by card overseas or use a foreign ATM, the screen may ask whether you want to be charged in AUD or the local currency. Choosing AUD lets the merchant's or ATM operator's provider set the exchange rate, which usually includes an extra markup. Always choose the local currency (yen, baht, euros and so on) and let your card handle the conversion.

2. What travel insurance traps should Australians look out for?

Common traps include travelling against Smartraveller advice, riding scooters without the right licence, and not declaring pre-existing medical conditions.

The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) runs Smartraveller, which gives destinations one of four advice levels. How each level affects your insurance depends on your insurer and policy.

Smartraveller advice levels – what to check in your policy: Level 1: Exercise normal safety precautions ---> Usually covered (subject to policy terms) Level 2: Exercise a high degree of caution ---> Usually covered (subject to policy terms) Level 3: Reconsider your need to travel ---> Varies by insurer – check the PDS Level 4: Do not travel ---> Commonly excluded – check the PDS

Key things to check: 1. Smartraveller advice levels: If DFAT's advice for your destination is Level 4 ("Do not travel"), many policies won't cover you if you go anyway. Cover for Level 3 ("Reconsider your need to travel") varies widely, so read the Product Disclosure Statement (PDS) carefully before you buy. Advice levels can change, so check again before you leave. 2. Scooters and motorbikes: Hiring a scooter is common in many South-East Asian destinations. Smartraveller notes that in Indonesia, for example, you need an Indonesian licence or an International Driving Permit (IDP) in addition to your Australian licence, and the licence must be right for the vehicle. Many insurers won't pay claims if you weren't correctly licenced. For Bali-specific tips, see our Bali travel cost per day guide. 3. Medical evacuation: Smartraveller is clear that the Australian Government won't pay for overseas medical costs or evacuation, and that without insurance you might have to pay thousands of dollars up front for medical care. Medical evacuation can be extremely expensive, so make sure your policy covers it. 4. Alcohol and drugs: Many policies exclude claims for incidents that happen while you're under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Check the exact wording in your PDS.

3. How do vouchers, price-beat guarantees and frequent flyer points cut airfares?

Flights are a big part of any holiday budget. Price-beat guarantees, vouchers and points can all help bring the cost down.

Under the Jetstar Price Beat Guarantee, if you find a cheaper comparable fare on another airline's own website, Jetstar says it will beat that fare by 10%. As of October 2026, the competing flight must depart within 90 minutes of the Jetstar flight for domestic flights, flights between Australia and New Zealand, and flights solely within Asia, or within 6 hours for other international flights. Other conditions apply – for example, it applies to Starter fares, the flight must depart at least 7 days after you apply, and fares on third-party travel agent sites don't count.

To stretch your flight budget further: * Combine vouchers with sales: Keep an eye out for airline credit vouchers, travel gift cards and credit card travel credits. Using them during major airline sales can cut your out-of-pocket costs – check the voucher terms first, as some can't be used on sale fares. * Compare routes and hubs: Fares vary depending on routing. If you're comparing destinations around Asia, see our guide to the cheapest countries to visit from Australia on a budget to find routes with plenty of low-cost competition. * Use points wisely: When redeeming Qantas Frequent Flyer or Velocity points, reward seats generally give better value per point than using points to pay for a cash fare.

4. What upfront admin costs should Australians budget for?

Budget for passport fees, visas, local transport passes and travel health costs before you go.

The biggest upfront admin cost is usually your passport. According to the Australian Passport Office, as of 1 January 2026 a standard 10-year adult passport costs $422. If you need it urgently, the Priority Processing fee is an extra $308 – bringing the total to $730.

Australian passport fees as of 1 January 2026 (Australian Passport Office): • Adult 10-year passport: $422 • Child 5-year passport: $213 • Priority Processing fee: +$308 • Overseas application fee (adult): +$189

Check the Australian Passport Office website for the latest fees and processing options before you apply.

Beyond your passport, your pre-departure checklist should cover: * Visas and tourist taxes: Bali, for example, requires a Visa on Arrival plus the Bali tourist levy, and some countries require an electronic travel authorisation (such as the US ESTA). Always use official government websites rather than third-party sites that charge extra service fees. * Transport passes: Some rail passes and transport cards can be bought or set up before you leave. If you're heading to Japan, read our guide on how to travel Japan on a budget for tips on rail passes and IC cards like Suica and Pasmo. * Travel health: Check Smartraveller's health advice for your destination and talk to your GP or a travel clinic about vaccinations and medication. Consultations and travel vaccines may not be fully covered by Medicare.

5. How can Australians build a flexible daily budget?

Split your spending into fixed costs (accommodation and pre-booked transport) and variable costs (food, activities and shopping), and keep a buffer for currency swings.

  1. Try a simple split: For example, put about 50% of your budget toward fixed essentials (flights, accommodation, insurance), 30% toward daily spending (food, local transport, entry tickets) and keep 20% for shopping, spontaneous activities and surprises. Adjust it to suit your trip.
  2. Use banking sub-accounts: Many banking apps let you set up separate savings "buckets". Moving each day's allowance into your spending account helps keep your daily spend visible.
  3. Allow for exchange rate changes: Rates move constantly. If you're planning months ahead, budget using a slightly less favourable exchange rate than today's (say, about 5% worse) so a dip in the Aussie dollar doesn't blow your budget.
